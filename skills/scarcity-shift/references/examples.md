# Worked examples

Use these for calibration: how strict to be, how long each section runs, and how classification differs from quality. They aren't templates to copy.

## Example 1: a faceless digital product (full evaluation)

**Profile summary:** senior software engineer with a full-time job. A few hours a week. Wants to stay anonymous: no face, no filming. Low-touch customer contact only. Small budget. No audience.

**Idea:** a $17 PDF kit that helps adult children set up their parents' phones before a holiday visit. Marketed with AI-assisted short videos on TikTok and YouTube Shorts from a new faceless account.

```
SCARCITY SHIFT VERDICT
Idea: $17 phone-setup PDF kit, sold through faceless short videos
Classification: Laboratory
Verdict: Run a bounded experiment
Scarce asset: none yet
Smallest honest test: hand the kit to 5–10 people with aging parents and watch what they do
```

1. **Primary classification:** Laboratory. It tests whether useful-but-unexciting content can find buyers through feeds. It isn't a business yet, because there's no evidence of demand.
2. **Economic thesis:** worried adult children will pay $17 to save an awkward afternoon of tech support, if they find the kit at the right moment.
3. **Steelman:** the pain is real and seasonal. Existing guides are written for seniors, not for the adult child doing the setup. The kit is cheap to make and to update.
4. **Red team:** a new faceless account has no distribution. Feeds reward entertainment, and settings tips aren't entertaining. Free guides and videos already cover each tip.
5. **Constraint conflicts:** no face and no filming limits the formats that hold attention on short-video feeds. Otherwise compatible.
6. **What became abundant:** written guides, scripts, and short videos about phone settings.
7. **What remains scarce:** the buyer's attention at the right moment, and trust that this guide is better than free ones.
8. **Distribution route:** borrowed, through TikTok, Shorts, and Pinterest. But "an algorithm is not a demand strategy." There's no owned or embedded route.
9. **Diffusion test:** negative. Better agents let anyone produce this kit, and let the parent's own phone assistant answer the questions directly.
10. **Economic asymmetry:** weak. Production cost collapsed for everyone, and free substitutes push the price toward zero.
11. **Ownership boundary:** weak. The kit hands off information. Nobody becomes accountable for the parent's phone actually being set up.
12. **Compounding asset:** little. Videos and the PDF are easy to regenerate. Learning about the audience is the only thing that accumulates.
13. **Attention-adjusted return:** cheap in money, but expensive in attention: daily posting, metrics, and the emotional cost of public results.
14. **Smallest honest test:** skip the feeds. Give the kit to 5–10 people you know with aging parents. Watch whether they use it, forward it, or say they'd pay.
15. **Explicit stop conditions:** stop if fewer than 2 of 10 testers use it unprompted, or if feed posts still show 0 sales after the holiday window.
16. **Decisive verdict:** run a bounded experiment. Treat it as a lab about distribution with a firm end date, not as a business.

**What happened:** after two days, about 420 short-video views, 6 product page views, and 0 sales. Most viewers left within the first second. The framework's red team was right: production was easy, and distribution was the whole problem.

## Example 2: a plane-tracking camera (verdict card)

**Profile summary:** engineer and hobby photographer who lives under an airport approach path.

**Idea:** an auto-tracking camera that films planes overhead and labels each one with live flight data, then sells the data or streams it.

```
SCARCITY SHIFT VERDICT
Idea: auto-tracking camera that labels planes over the house
Classification: Art / Creative Play
Verdict: Keep as art or creative play
Scarce asset: the build story and the person's reputation from sharing it
Smallest honest test: build a weekend prototype and post the write-up
```

Why it isn't a business: flight-tracking data is already a commodity. Global networks give it away to anyone who feeds them data from a cheap receiver. Popular plane-spotting streams film runways, not overhead approaches. The real value is a fun build that shows off skill, which also makes it good Professional Investment if written up publicly. Note that a live stream could reveal roughly where the person lives.

## Example 3: first-person household video for robot training data (verdict card)

**Profile summary:** has a home and time on weekends. Values privacy for their family.

**Idea:** wear a head camera while doing chores and sell the footage to robotics companies as training data.

```
SCARCITY SHIFT VERDICT
Idea: sell first-person chore footage as robot training data
Classification: Gentle Cash Side Hustle
Verdict: Kill or archive
Scarce asset: none (the buyer sets the price for interchangeable footage)
Smallest honest test: none recommended under this profile
```

Why: demand is real, but any household can supply it, so it pays like gig work. It also films the inside of the home and family members, which collides with the privacy constraint. Under a different profile, such as someone with unusual physical tasks or environments, it could be a reasonable gentle side hustle.
